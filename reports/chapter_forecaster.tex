\section{Introduction}

The digital twin of Chapter~\ref{ch:twin} consumes an \emph{offered load} and reports what
a UPF would do at that load. It does not say where the load comes from. This chapter builds
the component that does: a spatio-temporal forecaster that predicts near-future traffic for
each of the $K$ geographic service areas into which a city's base stations are grouped.

As in the rest of this thesis, it is worth being precise about scope, because the system is
deliberately decomposed into independent parts:

\begin{description}
  \item[The forecaster is not a calibrator.] It emits a \emph{dimensionless} per-cluster load
  $\hat{d}$ (the sum of member-station loads, each normalised to $[0,1]$). The mapping to physical Gbps is the scenario constant $\alpha$ owned by
  the twin (Chapter~\ref{ch:twin}, Eq.~1.1); it is applied \emph{after} forecasting and never
  enters training. One trained forecaster therefore serves every capacity scenario.
  \item[The forecaster is not a controller.] It predicts load; it does not choose a UPF
  configuration. Controllers consume the forecast (and its error bar) but are out of scope here
  (Chapter~\ref{ch:controllers}).
\end{description}

This separation mirrors the repository structure: the forecaster is a standalone project whose
only coupling to the twin is a fixed file contract (Section~\ref{sec:fc-contract}).

The contribution of this chapter is not a new forecasting architecture but a documented spatial
pipeline and an evidence-based answer to three questions a system designer must settle before wiring a
forecaster to the twin. The pipeline (Sections~\ref{sec:fc-graph}--\ref{sec:fc-cluster}) turns raw
per-tile measurements into a signal on a graph of $K$ contiguous service areas: Voronoi attribution of
tiles to base stations, an adjacency graph over the stations, and a geographic clustering whose
structure is characterised in its own right. The questions are the following. First, \emph{which
forecaster} to use: Section~\ref{sec:comparison} benchmarks six models from a linear baseline to
established spatio-temporal graph networks under a common protocol, and finds the field tight, with a
simple, cheap model losing little to the most sophisticated. Second, at \emph{what spatial granularity}
one should forecast: is it better to aggregate base stations into service areas and forecast the
cluster signal directly (\emph{cluster-first}), or to forecast every base station and sum?
Section~\ref{sec:ablation} answers this with a controlled ablation and finds the cluster-first route
dramatically better --- aggregation \emph{creates} predictability by denoising sparse per-station
traffic. Third, whether the \emph{geographic} form of that aggregation matters for forecasting:
Section~\ref{sec:partition} replaces the service areas by $k$-means and by random groupings of the
same sizes, and shows that the predictability comes from pooling stations rather than from their
contiguity --- geography is a deployment requirement whose cost in forecast error can be measured. The
headline finding is that \emph{what} is forecast matters far more than \emph{which model} forecasts it.

\paragraph{Place in the thesis.} Within this thesis's theme of \emph{augmented
observability} for the 5/6G data plane, the forecaster extends the observability
envelope along its \emph{temporal} axis. The profiling campaign made per-function
power and QoS observable at the present instant; the forecaster pushes the
horizon forward, making the \emph{near future} of per-cluster load observable one
control step ahead. A controller that could see only the present would have to
react to congestion after it arrived; forecasting is what lets the downstream
twin and controllers act before the load does. Its accuracy is therefore not a
private metric but part of the observability contract: the twin sizes its
anti-flapping band directly from the forecast error, so forecast quality
propagates into control behaviour (Section~\ref{sec:fc-contract}).

The chapter proceeds as follows. Section~\ref{sec:fc-graph} describes the data and builds the spatial
substrate --- Voronoi cells and the base-station graph --- and characterises the traffic on it.
Section~\ref{sec:fc-cluster} clusters the base stations into service areas and derives the forecasting
graph. Section~\ref{sec:fc-model} introduces the forecasting models, Section~\ref{sec:fc-contract}
states the output contract consumed by the twin, and Section~\ref{sec:fc-method} defines the
evaluation methodology and the skill metric. Section~\ref{sec:comparison} benchmarks the models and
selects one; Section~\ref{sec:fc-anatomy} dissects its error by cluster, lead time and load regime;
Sections~\ref{sec:graphsize}--\ref{sec:generalization} characterise it across cluster counts and
traffic types. Sections~\ref{sec:ablation} and~\ref{sec:partition} then test the two spatial design
decisions themselves --- forecasting at the cluster level, and clustering geographically.
Section~\ref{sec:fc-summary} summarises.

\section{Data and Spatial Substrate}
\label{sec:fc-graph}

Before any forecasting model is involved, three steps turn raw operator measurements into a signal
on a graph: the measurements are normalised, attributed to base stations, and the base stations are
connected into an adjacency graph. This section documents those steps and characterises the result,
because the properties established here --- how sparse a single station is, how fast correlation
decays in space --- are what the design decisions of the following sections rest on.

\subsection{Dataset and normalisation}

\paragraph{Dataset.} The forecaster is driven by NetMob 2023, the same source the twin's
NetMob adapter targets: per-tile downlink traffic for Lyon over 77 days
(2019-03-16 to 2019-05-31) at 15-minute resolution (96 slots/day; $T=7388$ slots, one hour being absent at the daylight-saving change), for the services
Netflix, YouTube, and DailyMotion. The spatial unit is a $100\,\mathrm{m}\times100\,\mathrm{m}$ tile; the
Lyon footprint comprises 54\,013 tiles covering 539\,km$^2$. The raw values are privacy-preserving
aggregate indicators with \emph{no physical unit}; this is the structural reason the pipeline is
normalised and the twin needs $\alpha$.

\paragraph{Normalisation.} Tile traffic is aggregated to base stations and divided by a single
global maximum computed over all services,
\begin{equation}
  \hat{d} \;=\; \frac{\text{bs\_tile\_sum}}{\text{global\_max}},
  \qquad \text{global\_max} = 1.482\times10^{8},
\end{equation}
so that $\hat{d}\in[0,1]$ for every base station. A \emph{single} normaliser (rather than one per
service) is used precisely so that the cross-service ratios are preserved and cluster signals can be
formed by direct summation: the mean city-wide load of Netflix ($4.39$) is close to that of YouTube
($4.28$) and $\approx 4.7\times$ that of DailyMotion ($0.94$), differences the normalisation retains.
Note that only the \emph{per-station} load is bounded by one; a cluster signal is a sum over member
stations and routinely exceeds it (Section~\ref{sec:fc-cluster}).

\paragraph{Data quality.} The trace contains one collection outage, on 12~May 2019, during which
the recorded load is near zero from 01:00 to 18:00. The series is used as recorded. Under the
chronological split of Section~\ref{sec:fc-method} the outage falls entirely inside the validation
window: it touches neither the training data nor any reported test metric, and it perturbs the
early-stopping criterion identically for every model.

\subsection{From tiles to base stations: Voronoi attribution}

NetMob reports traffic per tile, whereas the network is operated per site. Tiles are attributed to
sites with a Voronoi tessellation built from the 965 distinct gNodeB locations inside the Lyon
footprint (ANFR Cartoradio; co-located antennas are merged). The tessellation is computed in a local
metric projection and each cell is clipped to the city footprint; a tile is assigned to the cell that
contains its centroid, and a station's load is the sum over its tiles. The Voronoi cell is the
standard zero-information approximation of a coverage area: in the absence of antenna azimuths, powers
and propagation data, it assigns every location to its nearest site.

Figure~\ref{fig:fc-voronoi}(a) shows the resulting cells. Their size varies by nearly three orders of
magnitude, following the site density: the median cell covers 0.34\,km$^2$ (34 tiles), the mean
0.56\,km$^2$, and the largest peripheral cell 7.5\,km$^2$ (Figure~\ref{fig:fc-spstats}(a)). Traffic
is concentrated where the cells are smallest: the load density of the central cells exceeds that of
the periphery by up to two orders of magnitude.

\subsection{The base-station graph}

Two base stations are adjacent when their clipped Voronoi cells share a border of at least 1\,m; the
edge is weighted by the inverse of the distance between the two sites. The resulting graph
(Figure~\ref{fig:fc-voronoi}(b)) has 965 nodes and 2\,772 undirected edges and is connected. As
expected of a planar tessellation its degree distribution is narrow (mean 5.7, median 6, range 1--11;
Figure~\ref{fig:fc-spstats}(b)), while edge lengths are right-skewed (median 0.60\,km, 90th percentile
1.48\,km, maximum 4.1\,km; Figure~\ref{fig:fc-spstats}(c)), again reflecting the dense centre and
sparse periphery. This graph plays two roles: it is the contiguity constraint for the clustering of
Section~\ref{sec:fc-cluster}, and it is the fine graph from which every coarse forecasting graph is
derived.

\subsection{Traffic characterisation}
\label{sec:fc-traffic}

Four properties of the signal, summarised in Figure~\ref{fig:fc-traffic}, shape everything that
follows.

\emph{The city-level signal is strongly diurnal.} All three services share a night-time trough near
05:00 and differ in their daytime shape (Figure~\ref{fig:fc-traffic}(a)): Netflix peaks sharply in the
late evening at roughly twice its mean, YouTube plateaus through the afternoon and evening, and
DailyMotion shows a pronounced weekday midday peak. Weekend profiles rise later in the morning.

\emph{A single base station is sparse.} For Netflix, 88\% of all station-slots carry
$\hat d<0.01$, and the median station is below that level 93\% of the time; for DailyMotion the
figure is 99\% (Figure~\ref{fig:fc-traffic}(b)). Per-station traffic is thus a sequence of isolated
bursts on a near-zero floor.

\emph{Aggregation restores temporal structure.} The autocorrelation of a typical station drops to
0.58 after one 15-minute slot and reaches only 0.24 at the 24-hour lag; for the ten-cluster signals
of Section~\ref{sec:fc-cluster} the corresponding values are 0.80 and 0.60, and for the city
aggregate 0.96 and 0.86 (Figure~\ref{fig:fc-traffic}(c)). The daily rhythm that makes traffic
forecastable is barely present at a single station and emerges only as stations are summed --- the
observation that the ablation of Section~\ref{sec:ablation} turns into a controlled result.

\emph{Correlation is local.} The median correlation between two stations is 0.74 when they are
adjacent, 0.44 at two hops and 0.31 at three, and reaches the city-wide background level of
$\approx 0.23$ by about six hops (Figure~\ref{fig:fc-traffic}(d)). Spatial dependence therefore
exists, is short-ranged, and is aligned with the Voronoi adjacency --- which motivates both grouping
\emph{neighbouring} stations and giving the forecaster a graph.

\section{Service-Area Clustering}
\label{sec:fc-cluster}

With 965 base stations, neither per-station control nor --- as Section~\ref{sec:ablation} will show ---
per-station forecasting is viable. The stations are therefore grouped into $K$ service areas, each of
which is the catchment of one edge UPF site and one node of the forecasting graph.

\subsection{Requirements and method}

A partition into service areas must satisfy three requirements that follow from the deployment rather
than from forecasting. (i)~\emph{Contiguity}: a service area is the set of cells anchored to one user
plane function, so it must be a single connected region. (ii)~\emph{Compactness}: the distance from a
site to its UPF bounds the transport latency, so areas should be geographically tight.
(iii)~\emph{Service independence}: the partition describes infrastructure and must not change with
the traffic type being forecast.

These requirements are met by SKATER~\cite{assuncao2006skater}, a divisive regionalisation method
based on pruning a spanning tree of the adjacency graph. A minimum spanning tree of the base-station graph is built, with edges weighted by inter-site
distance; removing an edge from a tree splits it into two connected subtrees, so any set of $K-1$
edge removals yields $K$ contiguous regions by construction. The edges are removed greedily: at each
step the method cuts the edge that most reduces the within-region sum of squared deviations of the
site coordinates from their region centroid,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{SSD} \;=\; \sum_{k=1}^{K}\;\sum_{i\in\mathcal{C}_k} \bigl\lVert \mathbf{x}_i-\bar{\mathbf{x}}_k \bigr\rVert^2 ,
\end{equation}
subject to both resulting regions containing at least three base stations. The procedure is
deterministic and uses coordinates only, so requirement~(iii) holds trivially. Because each step
splits exactly one existing region, the partitions are \emph{nested} across $K$: the service areas at
$K{+}1$ refine those at $K$, which makes results at different $K$ directly comparable. One
implementation detail should be noted: distances and deviations are evaluated in geographic
coordinates (degrees) rather than in a metric projection, which at Lyon's latitude under-weights
east--west separations by a factor of about 0.70 relative to north--south ones.

Two alternatives were considered. Plain $k$-means on site coordinates yields compact areas but
ignores the adjacency graph, so contiguity is not guaranteed; it is kept as a comparison in
Section~\ref{sec:partition}. An attributed spectral clustering that mixed geographic affinity with
load-magnitude and temporal-shape features was explored in an earlier iteration and abandoned: the
traffic-feature terms produced spatially scattered areas and singletons, and made the partition depend
on the service, violating requirements (i) and (iii).

\subsection{Resulting service areas}

Figure~\ref{fig:fc-cmaps} shows the service areas for three values of $K$, and
Table~\ref{tab:fc-clusters} summarises their structure over the full range studied. Every cluster is
connected at every $K$, and compactness improves steadily, the mean distance from a station to its
cluster centroid falling from 4.1\,km at $K{=}4$ to 0.7\,km at $K{=}100$.

Two properties of the partition deserve emphasis, because they propagate to the twin and the
controllers.

\emph{The service areas are compact but not balanced.} SKATER minimises geographic dispersion, not
load or membership imbalance, and Lyon's site density is strongly peaked at the centre. At $K{=}10$
(Figure~\ref{fig:fc-k10}) the central cluster contains 297 of the 965 stations and carries 36\% of the
load, with a mean of $\hat d=1.57$ and a 99th percentile of 4.3, while the two smallest peripheral clusters
(36 and 42 stations) average 0.14 and 0.10; the coefficient of variation of cluster load is close to one at
every $K$. This imbalance is a feature of the city, not an artefact to be removed: it is what makes
the downstream configuration decision non-trivial, since clusters differ by an order of magnitude in
the capacity they require. It also means that pooled error metrics are dominated by the few heavy
clusters, which is why Section~\ref{sec:fc-anatomy} reports errors per cluster as well.

\emph{Geographic clusters are more coherent than chance, increasingly so as they shrink.} The mean
correlation between two stations of the same cluster rises from 0.31 at $K{=}4$ to 0.61 at $K{=}100$,
against 0.24 for size-matched random groupings at every $K$ (Table~\ref{tab:fc-clusters}). This is the
short-range correlation of Figure~\ref{fig:fc-traffic}(d) seen through the partition: the tighter the
area, the more its members move together.

\subsection{Coarsening to the forecasting graph}

Each cluster becomes one graph node. Two clusters are connected when any member base station of one is
Voronoi-adjacent to any member of the other, and the edge weight is the mean geographic affinity
(a Gaussian kernel on site coordinates) over those cross-border station pairs; self-loops of unit
weight are added. The node signal at each slot is the \emph{sum} of the member stations' $\hat{d}$,
\begin{equation}
  \hat d_k(t) \;=\; \sum_{i\in\mathcal{C}_k} \hat d_i(t),
\end{equation}
so traffic is conserved exactly: the cluster signals sum to the city total at every slot, which the
pipeline verifies numerically. The result is a $K$-node graph and a $(K\times T)$ signal matrix. The
coarse graph is sparse --- 18 edges at $K{=}10$, a mean degree of 3.6 --- and remains so as $K$ grows
(Table~\ref{tab:fc-clusters}). The complete pipeline is summarised in Figure~\ref{fig:fc-pipeline}.

\section{Forecasting Models}
\label{sec:fc-model}

{\sloppy Every model predicts, for all nodes simultaneously, the next $H=4$ slots (1\,h) from the previous
$L=96$ slots (24\,h): input $(B,L,N)$ of normalised loads (one feature per node) plus, for graph
models, the static graph $(\text{edge\_index},\text{edge\_attr})$; output $(B,H,N)$, with $N=K$
clusters at the cluster level. To answer ``which forecaster?'' without favouring any paradigm, the
benchmark of Section~\ref{sec:comparison} spans, under one training protocol:\par}

\begin{description}
  \item[Naive floors.] \emph{Persistence} (repeat the last value) and \emph{seasonal-naive} (the same
  slot 24\,h earlier) --- the references the skill metric is measured against.
  \item[DLinear] \cite{zeng2023dlinear} --- a channel-independent trend/seasonal decomposition with two
  linear maps; the ``is anything deep needed?'' baseline.
  \item[GRU (no graph)] --- a per-node gated recurrent encoder; the purely temporal model, and the
  natural ablation of the graph (any graph model's edge over it is the value of spatial information).
  \item[DCRNN] \cite{li2018dcrnn} --- diffusion-convolutional recurrence; spatial mixing fused into a
  GRU cell over the given graph.
  \item[Graph WaveNet] \cite{wu2019gwn} --- dilated temporal convolutions with a learnable adjacency.
  \item[AGCRN] \cite{bai2020agcrn} --- a node-adaptive GRU over an adjacency the model \emph{learns}
  from data, ignoring the supplied graph (a test of whether a better graph than Voronoi exists).
  \item[GAT--GRU (in-house)] --- a per-node GRU temporal encoder followed by a two-layer graph-attention
  spatial mixer (4 heads) and a linear head; included as a reasonable in-house spatio-temporal
  baseline, not as a proposed method.
\end{description}

The graph models share the same coarse $K$-node graph; the GRU and DLinear ignore it. All are
\emph{compact reimplementations on a common harness}: results reflect each architecture's inductive
bias under identical data, budget, and protocol rather than its best-tuned reference performance ---
the appropriate footing for a controlled comparison.

\section{Outputs and the Twin Data Contract}
\label{sec:fc-contract}

{\sloppy The forecaster does not call the twin; it writes files the twin reads. For a chosen
$(\text{service},K)$ it exports seven artifacts into a flat directory consumed by the twin's
loader: test-window predictions and targets (\texttt{predictions\_test.npy},
\texttt{targets\_test.npy}), the full cluster signal (\texttt{cluster\_series.npy}), the
cluster$\rightarrow$base-station map and assignment table, the base-station coordinates, and the
evaluation summary (\texttt{forecast\_eval\_summary.json}). The arrays are in normalised
units of $\hat d$; the twin applies $\alpha$. Critically, the forecaster's reported mean absolute
error is part of the contract: the twin derives its anti-flapping hysteresis band as
$2\cdot\mathrm{MAE}_\text{forecast}$ (Chapter~\ref{ch:twin}, Eq.~1.6), so forecast accuracy
propagates directly into control behaviour.\par}

\section{Evaluation Methodology}
\label{sec:fc-method}

\paragraph{Protocol.} The data is split chronologically (70/15/15 train/val/test; no shuffling)
to prevent leakage. Models train with Adam, an L1 (MAE) objective, cosine learning-rate decay, and
early stopping on validation MAE (patience 15). Unless stated, results are averaged over three
seeds.

\paragraph{Metrics.} Forecasts are scored in $\hat d$ units with MAE, RMSE, and WAPE,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{WAPE} = 100\cdot\frac{\sum|\,y-\hat y\,|}{\sum|y|},
\end{equation}
which is used instead of MAPE because traffic is sparse and MAPE explodes on near-zero
denominators.

\paragraph{Skill, not raw error.} Absolute error is misleading across graph sizes: as $K$ grows,
each cluster aggregates fewer base stations, so its signal is burstier and \emph{intrinsically}
harder in relative terms even though its absolute magnitude shrinks. The model-independent quantity
is therefore \emph{skill} over a baseline,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{skill} = 1 - \frac{\mathrm{WAPE}_\text{model}}{\mathrm{WAPE}_\text{baseline}},
  \label{eq:skill}
\end{equation}
reported against persistence (last value) and seasonal-naive (same slot 24\,h earlier); a
historical-mean baseline is also computed. Skill isolates what the model contributes from how hard
the target intrinsically is.

Three findings stand out.

\emph{First, spatial information helps --- modestly.} Every model that uses the given graph or learns one
(Graph WaveNet, DCRNN, AGCRN) beats the graph-free GRU at both sizes ($0.253$/$0.258$/$0.243$ vs.\ $0.234$
at $K{=}10$): a real, repeatable $\sim$3\% WAPE reduction. But the gain is small and does not grow with $K$,
and \emph{learning} the adjacency (AGCRN) does not beat the given Voronoi graph --- so the ceiling is the
weak residual spatial signal at cluster level, not the graph's quality: once stations are pooled into
service areas, little is left for the inter-cluster graph to add.

\emph{Second, the field is tight and simple models lose little.} From DLinear ($0.199$) to the best model
($0.258$) the entire spread is $\sim$0.06 skill, and the no-graph GRU ($0.234$) is within $\sim$0.02 of the
best. Model choice barely moves the needle.

\emph{Third, accuracy--cost favours the simple.} DCRNN leads but is among the slowest; Graph WaveNet matches
it to within $0.005$ skill at roughly a tenth of the cost (its dilated convolutions parallelise where
DCRNN's recurrence does not); AGCRN is the costliest ($252$k parameters, $\sim$28\,min/run) for no gain; and
the in-house GAT--GRU does not lead. We therefore adopt \textbf{Graph WaveNet} as the deployed forecaster
(best accuracy--cost); a plain \textbf{GRU} would be a defensible simpler choice. The rest of the chapter
characterises Graph WaveNet, and Section~\ref{sec:ablation} shows the one decision the system is \emph{not}
insensitive to --- forecasting granularity --- dwarfs every model difference here.

\section{Anatomy of the Forecast Error}
\label{sec:fc-anatomy}

The benchmark compares models through one pooled number. A controller, however, consumes one forecast
per cluster, at a given lead time, and is most sensitive to errors when load is high. This section
therefore opens up the pooled figure for the selected model at the reference configuration
(Graph WaveNet, $K{=}10$, Netflix; three seeds, independently retrained with test predictions
retained: pooled WAPE 22.1\%, skill 0.249, consistent with Table~\ref{tab:fc-compare}).
Figure~\ref{fig:fc-examples} shows representative forecasts and Figure~\ref{fig:fc-diag} the three
breakdowns.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig_forecast_examples.pdf}
\caption{Observed and forecast load, 60 minutes ahead, over five days of the test window for the
largest, a mid-sized and the smallest-load cluster of the $K{=}10$ partition (Graph WaveNet, one seed).
The forecast tracks the diurnal envelope at every scale; short spikes, increasingly prominent as the
cluster shrinks, are not anticipated.}
\label{fig:fc-examples}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig_diagnostics.pdf}
\caption{Breakdown of the Graph WaveNet test error at $K{=}10$ (Netflix; mean over three seeds, error
bars $\pm1$ std). (a) Skill over persistence per cluster, clusters ordered by decreasing mean load.
(b) WAPE by lead time, against both naive baselines. (c) WAPE by quartile of the realised load,
computed per cluster.}
\label{fig:fc-diag}
\end{figure}

\emph{The advantage is uniform across clusters.} Raw WAPE varies widely between clusters, from
16\% for the central cluster to 40\% for the smallest-membership one, mirroring the size effect of
Section~\ref{sec:graphsize}. Skill does not: it lies between 0.22 and 0.29 for all ten clusters
(Figure~\ref{fig:fc-diag}(a)), with no cluster on which the model fails to improve on persistence
and no systematic dependence on cluster load. The pooled figure is therefore representative, not an
average over good and bad areas, despite the load imbalance documented in
Section~\ref{sec:fc-cluster}.

\emph{The advantage grows with lead time.} Persistence degrades quickly as the horizon lengthens
(WAPE 23.5\% at 15 minutes, 34.5\% at 60), whereas the model degrades slowly (20.1\% to 23.7\%), so
skill rises from 0.14 at 15 minutes to 0.31 at 60 (Figure~\ref{fig:fc-diag}(b)). At 60 minutes
persistence is already worse than the seasonal-naive forecast. The model is thus most valuable exactly
where a controller needs it: a configuration change has to be decided ahead of the load it will
serve, so it is the far end of the horizon, not the next slot, that the decision depends on.

\emph{Peaks are under-forecast.} Relative error is highest in the lowest load quartile (34.8\%), where
absolute loads are small, and lowest in the third (17.6\%); in the top quartile it is 22.1\%, against
25.6\% for persistence, so the margin over persistence narrows from 0.29--0.35 in the lower three
quartiles to 0.14 at peak (Figure~\ref{fig:fc-diag}(c)). More important than the size of the error is
its sign. The forecast is biased upward at low load ($+11\%$ in the bottom quartile) and downward at
high load ($-17\%$ in the top quartile, $-35\%$ over the top 1\% of slots): it regresses toward the
diurnal envelope and misses the short spikes visible in Figure~\ref{fig:fc-examples}. This is the
expected behaviour of a model trained with an absolute-error objective, which targets the conditional
median of a right-skewed quantity. It matters downstream, because under-forecasting a peak is the
costly direction for a capacity decision. The twin's hysteresis band, sized from the forecast MAE
(Section~\ref{sec:fc-contract}), absorbs part of it; a quantile or asymmetric training objective
would address it at the source and is the most direct improvement left open by this chapter.

\section{Cluster-First vs.\ Per-Base-Station: Why Aggregate?}
\label{sec:ablation}

The preceding sections chose a model (Graph WaveNet) and showed it forecasts cluster load robustly across
graph sizes and traffic types. All of it rests on one prior decision: that we forecast at the \emph{cluster}
level at all. The MEC deployment already dictates clusters --- but is aggregating first also a
\emph{forecasting} advantage, or merely a convenience we would pay for in accuracy? This section tests
that decision head-on.

Section~\ref{sec:graphsize} cannot answer it --- it scores each $K$ against its own (changing) target. A
controlled test instead fixes the delivered target (the $K{=}10$ cluster signal) and compares two routes to
it:
\begin{description}
  \item[Route A (cluster-first).] Aggregate to $K{=}10$ clusters; forecast the cluster signals directly.
  \item[Route B (per-base-station).] Forecast all 965 base stations; \emph{sum} the predictions into the
  same 10 clusters.
\end{description}
Both are scored on the identical $K{=}10$ target --- the 965-station series sums exactly to the cluster
series (verified to floating-point error) --- so the comparison isolates \emph{when} aggregation happens.
The route is run with the graph-free GRU and a sparse graph-attention STGNN; the dense-adjacency models
(Graph WaveNet, DCRNN, AGCRN) do not even scale to 965 nodes, which is itself telling.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.62\textwidth]{figures/fig_ablation.pdf}
\caption{Forecasting the aggregated cluster signal directly (cluster-first) versus forecasting 965 base
stations and summing (per-base-station), scored on the same $K{=}10$ target. The per-station route
collapses to persistence-level skill ($\approx 0$); cluster-first reaches $0.21$--$0.23$ (mean over three
seeds, error bars $\pm1$ std).}
\label{fig:fc-ablation}
\end{figure}

The result (Figure~\ref{fig:fc-ablation}) is decisive: forecasting at the base-station level and summing
yields a cluster forecast \emph{no better than persistence} (skill $-0.02$ to $-0.03$), whereas forecasting
the cluster signal directly reaches $0.21$--$0.23$ --- consistent across both models and all three seeds.
The mechanism is \emph{denoising by aggregation}: per-station traffic is extremely sparse (for Netflix,
$88\%$ of station-slots are below $0.01$; Section~\ref{sec:fc-traffic}), so 965 such series are nearly unpredictable individually,
and a sum of persistence-level forecasts is itself persistence-level. The \emph{sum}, by contrast, is a
smooth, strongly diurnal signal that is easy to forecast.

This reframes the role of aggregation. Pooling stations before forecasting is not a loss of information
to be tolerated for the sake of tractability: it is what makes the problem forecastable at all, and no
model choice comes close to the gap seen here. \textbf{Cluster-first is a forecasting advantage, not
merely a tractability convenience.} (A far larger per-station model might recover some skill, but the
gap --- $0.22$ versus $\approx 0$ --- is too large to plausibly close, and is consistent with the
per-station sparsity.) What the ablation does not establish is that the aggregation has to be
\emph{geographic}; that is a separate question, taken up next.

\section{Does the Partition Matter? Geographic vs.\ Random Aggregation}
\label{sec:partition}

The ablation shows that stations must be pooled before forecasting. It is tempting to conclude that
the \emph{geographic} pooling of Section~\ref{sec:fc-cluster} is what creates the predictable signal ---
that grouping neighbouring, correlated stations is how spatial structure is exploited. That is a
separate claim, and it can be tested by holding everything fixed (the 965 station series, $K$, the
models, the protocol) and changing only how the stations are partitioned:

\begin{description}
  \item[SKATER.] The contiguous service areas used throughout this chapter.
  \item[$k$-means.] $k$-means on projected site coordinates: geographic and compact, but with no
  contiguity constraint.
  \item[Random.] The SKATER labels permuted over the stations: the same $K$ and exactly the same
  cluster sizes, but no geography. Three independent permutations are drawn.
\end{description}

Each partition is coarsened by the rule of Section~\ref{sec:fc-cluster} and forecast with the
graph-free GRU and with Graph WaveNet under the protocol of Section~\ref{sec:fc-method}, at
$K{=}10$ and $K{=}50$ (three seeds per partition; 60 training runs). As a check on the harness, the
re-derived SKATER coarsening reproduces the pipeline's stored cluster signals and coarse graph
exactly. Table~\ref{tab:fc-partition} and Figure~\ref{fig:fc-partition} report the outcome.

\begin{table}[t]
\centering

(3 seeds; $3\times3$ for the random partitions).}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig_partition_control.pdf}

\emph{Geography is not what makes the signal predictable.} The random partition is not harder to
forecast; it is markedly easier. At $K{=}10$ Graph WaveNet reaches a WAPE of 14.6\% on random
groups against 22.2\% on the SKATER areas, and 20.8\% against 34.2\% at $K{=}50$. The targets
themselves are smoother --- persistence alone scores 20.3\% on random groups and 29.5\% on SKATER
areas at $K{=}10$ --- and the model's skill over persistence is, if anything, higher (0.285
against 0.248). The predictability documented in Section~\ref{sec:ablation} therefore comes from
\emph{pooling many stations}, not from pooling \emph{neighbouring} ones. The explanation is
statistical. A sum of weakly correlated bursty series averages its fluctuations out; a sum of
strongly correlated ones does not. Stations in a geographic cluster are positively correlated
($\bar\rho_\text{in}=0.33$ at $K{=}10$, 0.50 at $K{=}50$, against 0.24 for random groups; recall that
adjacent stations correlate at 0.74), so a contiguous area retains local fluctuations that a
city-wide sample of the same size cancels. It would therefore be wrong
to say that aggregation ``exploits'' spatial structure: spatial correlation works \emph{against} the
smoothing that aggregation provides.

\emph{But a random partition forecasts nothing useful.} The reason random groups are so smooth is
that each one is a scaled-down replica of the whole city: their signals correlate at 0.93 with one
another at $K{=}10$ (0.76 at $K{=}50$), against 0.65 (0.43) for the service areas
(Figure~\ref{fig:fc-partition}(c)). Ten such forecasts carry little more than one forecast of the
city total, and they do not correspond to anything a user plane function can serve: none of the
random clusters is contiguous, and their members lie on average 6.4\,km from the centroid, more than
twice the 2.7\,km of a SKATER area. The quantity the system needs is the load \emph{of a place}, and
that is intrinsically harder to predict than the load of a random sample. The difference --- about
8 WAPE points at $K{=}10$ and 13 at $K{=}50$ --- is the price of locality. It is a property of the
problem, not a deficiency of the pipeline, and it also accounts for the rise of raw error with $K$ in
Section~\ref{sec:graphsize}: smaller areas pool fewer and more strongly correlated stations.

\emph{Among geographic partitions the method matters little.} $k$-means and SKATER produce areas of
similar compactness and coherence, and their forecast quality differs by no more than 0.006 skill at
either size, in opposite directions (Graph WaveNet 0.244 against 0.248 at $K{=}10$, 0.235 against
0.229 at $K{=}50$). On this site layout $k$-means also happened to return contiguous clusters in every case.
The choice of SKATER therefore rests on what it guarantees by construction --- contiguity, and
partitions that are nested across $K$ --- and not on a forecasting advantage. Finally, the graph is
only useful when it means something: Graph WaveNet improves on the graph-free GRU by about 0.01
skill on the two geographic partitions and by 0.003 on the random one at $K{=}10$, where the coarse
graph is complete and carries no information.

Two caveats apply. The control uses one service and two values of $K$; and one of the nine GRU runs
on a random partition stopped after four epochs (skill 0.03), which is why medians rather than
means are reported --- every run is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:fc-partition}.

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:fc-summary}

This chapter built the spatio-temporal forecaster that supplies the digital twin's offered load.
The principal points are:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Scope.} The forecaster emits a dimensionless per-cluster load $\hat d$; physical
  calibration ($\alpha$) and control are external. One trained model serves every capacity scenario.
  \item \textbf{A documented spatial substrate.} 54\,013 tiles are attributed to 965 base stations by
  Voronoi tessellation, giving a connected adjacency graph of 2\,772 edges. On it, single-station
  traffic is sparse (88\% of Netflix station-slots are near zero) and weakly autocorrelated, and
  correlation between stations is short-ranged, decaying to the city-wide background within about six
  hops.
  \item \textbf{Contiguous, compact, unbalanced service areas.} SKATER partitions the stations into
  $K$ connected areas that are nested across $K$ and independent of the service. The areas are
  compact (mean radius 2.7\,km at $K{=}10$) but far from balanced: one central cluster holds 31\% of
  the stations and 36\% of the load, a property of the city that the downstream control problem
  inherits.
  \item \textbf{Model choice barely matters; deploy simple.} Six forecasters from linear to learned-graph
  span only $\sim$0.06 skill; Graph WaveNet gives the best accuracy--cost trade-off and a plain GRU is
  near-best. The inter-cluster graph adds a small, repeatable gain ($\sim$3\% WAPE), and a learned
  adjacency (AGCRN) does not beat the Voronoi-derived one. Graph WaveNet is recommended for the twin.
  \item \textbf{The advantage is uniform across clusters, grows with lead time, and is weakest at
  peaks.} At $K{=}10$ skill lies between 0.22 and 0.29 for every cluster and rises from 0.14 at 15
  minutes to 0.31 at one hour; peaks are systematically under-forecast ($-17\%$ in the top load
  quartile), a consequence of the absolute-error objective and the main opening for improvement.
  \item \textbf{The problem is well-behaved.} Skill over persistence is near-constant across graph sizes
  ($K=4\!\rightarrow\!100$; rising raw WAPE reflects intrinsic small-cluster difficulty, not model decay)
  and holds across aggregate and sparse traffic types.
  \item \textbf{Cluster-first is a forecasting advantage (central result).} A controlled ablation on a
  fixed target shows that forecasting the aggregated cluster signal directly reaches $0.21$--$0.23$ skill,
  while forecasting all 965 base stations and summing is no better than persistence ($\approx 0$).
  Aggregation \emph{denoises} sparse per-station traffic into a predictable signal.
  \item \textbf{Predictability comes from pooling; geography is the requirement, and it has a price.}
  Random groupings of the same sizes are \emph{easier} to forecast than the service areas (WAPE 14.6\%
  against 22.2\% at $K{=}10$) because they average out the local correlation that contiguous areas
  retain --- but their signals are near-copies of the city total and map to no deployable area.
  Forecasting the load of a place is intrinsically harder than forecasting the load of a sample;
  among geographic partitions, SKATER and $k$-means forecast equally well, so SKATER is retained for
  its guaranteed contiguity.
\end{enumerate}
